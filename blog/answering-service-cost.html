How Much Does an Answering Service Cost in 2026?

By the Greetro team Published August 27, 2026 8 min read

The short answer: most traditional answering services bill roughly $1–$2 per minute or per call, which puts a typical small office somewhere between $100 and $500 a month. Virtual receptionist services with more capable agents usually sell monthly bundles that run a few hundred to over a thousand dollars. A full-time in-house receptionist costs far more — roughly $31,000–$46,000 a year in wages alone, and more with payroll taxes and benefits — while covering only about a quarter of the hours in a week. AI receptionists are the newest and cheapest tier: Greetro runs $49–$399 per month for 24/7 coverage.

Those are the headline numbers. The rest of this guide unpacks how each pricing model actually works, where the surprise charges hide, and how to run the math for your own office — because the “cheapest” option on paper is rarely the cheapest option once you count the calls it fails to convert.

The four ways businesses pay to answer the phone

Every phone-coverage option on the market is a variation on one of four models:

  • Traditional answering services — live operators who pick up under your business name and take messages, billed per minute or per call.
  • Virtual receptionist services — more capable remote receptionists who can book, answer questions, and transfer, sold as monthly minute bundles.
  • An in-house receptionist — an employee at your front desk, paid a wage plus everything that rides on top of a wage.
  • An AI receptionist — software that answers with a natural voice, books appointments, and logs every call, sold as a flat monthly subscription.

Traditional answering service pricing

Per-minute billing

The most common model. You pay for operator time, typically in the range of $1–$2 per minute, usually sold as a monthly bundle — say, 100 minutes for around $100–$200 — with overage rates above that. Two details matter enormously here. First, how the service rounds: many round each call up to the next full minute, so thirty 40-second calls can bill as thirty minutes even though the operators only spoke for twenty. Second, what counts as billable: some services start the clock while the caller is on hold, or bill for wrong numbers and robocalls.

Per-call billing

Instead of minutes, you pay a flat rate for each answered call — often somewhere between $1 and $3 depending on how much the operator is expected to do. Per-call pricing is predictable if your calls are long, and expensive if your line gets lots of quick calls, hang-ups, and spam, because every pickup costs the same.

Flat monthly plans

Some services quote a single monthly price for a volume tier. These are really per-minute plans with the math done for you — useful for budgeting, but read the tier limits carefully, because busy months can push you into overage rates that are meaningfully higher than the headline rate.

Virtual receptionist pricing

Virtual receptionist services sit a tier above message-taking: their agents follow your intake script, schedule appointments, and transfer calls. You pay for that capability. Plans commonly start around $200–$300 a month for a modest bundle of receptionist minutes and climb from there; a busy office that wants most calls handled end-to-end can easily spend $600 to well over $1,000 a month. Setup fees and charges for “extra” work like outbound confirmation calls are common enough that they belong in your comparison spreadsheet, not a footnote.

The full-time receptionist math

An in-house receptionist does far more than answer phones, so this is not a pure apples-to-apples comparison — but the phone-coverage math is still worth writing down.

In most US markets, receptionist wages run roughly $15–$22 an hour. At full time, that is about $31,000–$46,000 a year in wages. Employers also pay payroll taxes, and most add some mix of insurance, paid time off, and training, so many small businesses realistically budget $40,000–$55,000 a year for the seat — call it $3,300–$4,600 a month. Wages vary a lot by market: the same role costs meaningfully more in New York or Los Angeles than in Fargo or St. Cloud.

Here is the part the salary line hides: a full-time employee covers about 40 of the 168 hours in a week — roughly 24% — and that is before lunch breaks, sick days, vacations, and the simple reality that one person cannot answer two calls at once. Every call outside those hours still needs an answer from somewhere.

Hidden costs to check before you sign anything

  • Rounding rules — per-minute services that round up turn short calls into long invoices.
  • Billable hold and wrap-up time — some services bill time the caller spends on hold or the operator spends typing notes.
  • Spam and wrong numbers — ask whether robocalls count against your bundle.
  • After-hours and holiday premiums — live coverage at 2 a.m. often costs extra; software coverage does not.
  • Setup fees and contract length — month-to-month with a free trial tells you the provider expects to earn your renewal.

How to choose for your office

Start from your call log, not a price sheet. Count a normal week’s calls, note how many arrive outside business hours, and mark which ones were bookings or new-business inquiries. If most calls are routine and schedulable, an AI receptionist covers the most hours for the least money. If your calls are long, sensitive conversations, budget for humans — or run a hybrid, with your team answering the calls they can and an AI catching overflow and after-hours. And whichever way you lean, weigh the cost of coverage against the cost of the calls you currently miss — for most offices that number decides the debate.

Answering service cost FAQ

What does an answering service typically cost per month?

Most traditional services bill by the minute or by the call, and small offices typically land between about $100 and $500 a month depending on volume. Virtual receptionist services usually run higher — often a few hundred dollars a month for 100–300 minutes. Always confirm what counts as a billable minute.

Is an answering service cheaper than hiring a receptionist?

Almost always. A full-time receptionist costs roughly $31,000–$46,000 a year in wages before taxes and benefits, yet covers about 40 of the week’s 168 hours. Any service measured in hundreds a month is cheaper on paper — the real comparison is quality, capability, and what happens to the calls each option can’t handle.

What’s the difference between an answering service and a virtual receptionist?

The line is blurry, but broadly: answering services pick up and take messages from a script, while virtual receptionists can book appointments, answer real questions, and transfer calls. You pay more per minute for that capability.
